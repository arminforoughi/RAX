Application · Lab automation

One camera. Every tube in its rack.

A 5-axis arm with a single camera on its wrist maps the tubes lying on a bench, picks each one square to the table, stands it upright, and drops it into the rack for its cap colour. No demonstrations and no hand-placed coordinates.

It picks four in five.

An unedited run of the scripted pipeline: map the bench, pick each tube square to the table, stand it up and drop it into its rack. With this method the arm gets 80% success on the pick-up and 50% on the drop into the rack hole.

80%Pick-up success
50%Drop into the hole

The pick has a tolerance of about 5 mm and the camera clears it easily. The drop is the hard half: a 13 mm tube in a ~15 mm hole leaves about 1 mm of clearance, below what an open-loop move can hit. Closing that gap takes a contact search at the rim, not better pixels.

What's new

Six ideas that make it work

01

Map once, then tap tap

One sweep of the wrist camera across the bench, looking out and looking down, builds a map of every tube. Then the arm goes tube by tube in a single direction instead of searching back and forth.

3 bearings × 2 tilts · one pass
02

Pixel to world, through the arm

The camera rides the wrist, so its pose is always known from the arm's own kinematics. A cap's pixel becomes a ray, the ray meets the table, and the tube has a position in robot coordinates. Turning toward it is read off the model, never from an assumed sign convention.

FK camera pose · ray-to-plane cast
03

Read the body, not the cap

A cap is about as long as it is wide, so its shape says nothing about direction. RAX casts rays out of the cap and scores each by contrast against its neighbours; the tube body is the streak that stands out, on wood or on a dark mat. After turning the wrist it checks the picture rotated as predicted, and undoes a turn that was based on a bad read.

3° rays · contrast vs ±36° · median of 3 frames
04

Vision steers, IK drives

The sideways error in the image steers only the base, through a gain the arm measures with a small test turn. Every joint angle is solved on the arm's own model. A multi-start solver finds a grasp square to the table where a single start gives up at 70°, and the last centimetres are a straight vertical drop, so the fingers never sweep the tube away.

measured px/deg · multi-seed IK · fixed-angle descent
05

Stand it up, drop it in

Held from above, the tube lies across the jaws. Level the hand and it hangs vertical. The arm carries it high over the rack, lowers over a free hole it can reach, releases, and looks from above for a cap in that hole.

blue · green → black rack · red → silver rack
06

It knows when it failed

A grip is judged by the jaws: they close fully on air and stop short on a tube. While picking, a guardrail keeps the arm from ever turning toward the racks. Every attempt is tagged with what happened, and the tags are the labels of a dataset collected for free.

placed · missed hole · missed grasp · not found · unreachable
What we tried first

Why an end-to-end VLA didn't work.

Before the scripted pipeline we trained SmolVLA end to end on teleoperated demonstrations of the same task, recorded on an X250 arm: two cameras and six joints in, joint actions out. It didn't sort tubes reliably, and the reasons were in the data and the task, not the model size.

01

Too few examples of the rule

Blue and green go to the black rack, red to the silver one. The policy had to infer that rule from pixels, from about 43 episodes per colour. Green really had 22: 21 of its 43 were exact duplicates. A rule that fits in a three-row lookup table was being learned from a few dozen examples.

43 / 22 / 43 distinct episodes per colour
02

The instruction said nothing

Every frame of every episode carried the same sentence, "Pick up the blood tube and place it in the rack". The text told the policy nothing about which phase it was in, which tube to take or which rack to use, so the language half of the vision-language-action model had nothing to do.

1 instruction for ~69k frames
03

Noisy, fixed-length demonstrations

Episodes were cut at a fixed ~20 s, not when the task ended, so on average 18% of each one is the arm idling after the release. One in eleven taught the wrong thing: recording stopped while still holding the tube, the grasp was missing, or nothing was picked. And the training stack only loads one recording session at a time, so early runs saw 18–20 episodes, not 117.

11 of 117 bad · 18% dead time · 1 session per run
04

It can't see what it needs

Nothing in the policy's input says which holes in the rack are free, so it can't choose one. And the drop has about 1 mm of clearance. Imitation reproduces the average of the demonstrations, and averaging moves that differ by a few millimetres misses a 1 mm hole. That is a contact problem, not a pattern-matching one.

no occupancy input · ~1 mm clearance

So the pipeline splits the work: geometry, the arm's own kinematics and sensing do what they can do exactly, and learning goes where it pays off, in seeing the tubes. The VLA stays as a baseline to beat.

Next · in progress

Teleop data becomes a detector.

Today the caps are found by measured colour thresholds, and every change of light or bench means re-measuring. The next step replaces them with a small learned model, trained on data we already have: 200 teleoperation episodes, recorded for a policy, turned into a labelled detection set with almost no hand labelling.

A

Mine the episodes

Every 10th frame from the wrist and overhead cameras: thousands of images from this exact bench, lighting and set of tubes.

200 episodes · ~2–4k frames
B

Label with foundation models

An open-vocabulary detector finds each tube, a segmentation model cuts out its exact outline, and the outline gives a rotated box: position and direction together. The cap colour inside it gives the class.

open-vocab detection · segmentation · rotated boxes
C

Let the demos check the labels

At the frame where the gripper closes, the tube is known to be between the jaws. That confirms labels at every grasp, and tracking carries them back through the approach.

grasp frames as free ground truth
D

One small model, same pipeline

A rotated-box detector outputs each tube's position, angle and cap colour in one pass, and drops into the pipeline where the colour thresholds sit today. The geometry, the IK and the checks stay as they are.

oriented-box detector · real-time on the robot PC

The same move turns failed learning into useful learning: demonstrations that were not enough to train a whole policy are plenty to train the part of it that sees.

Beyond tubes

The same loop picks anything it can see.

Nothing in detect → locate → orient → solve → verify is specific to tubes. Swap the detector and the pipeline picks vials, cubes or parts, and places into trays or bins. Bringing a cap between the jaws and bringing a held tube over a hole are the same visual servo, used twice.
